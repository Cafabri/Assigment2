\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The triple-loop \texttt{ijk} multiplication of Assignment~1 was compared in C with three
optimised dense kernels (loop reordering \texttt{ikj}, cache blocking and a transposed
operand) and with a CSR sparse multiplication (Gustavson's algorithm), on the performance
cores of an Intel Core i7-13700KF (Windows~11), under a budget of 15~minutes per run.
Every kernel adds the products of each element in the same order, and all results were
bit-identical to \texttt{ijk} and agreed with NumPy and SciPy. For dense matrices,
\texttt{ikj} needed 1.6--8.6 times less time than \texttt{ijk} for $500 \le n \le 4000$
and the blocked kernel 1.7--16.4 times less; the blocked kernel kept a constant cost of
0.22~ns per multiply-add from $n = 500$ to $4000$, without the jump at $n = 2000$ seen in
Assignment~1. Compiler vectorisation (AVX2) made the blocked kernel a further 2.5--2.8
times faster, up to 45.8 times faster than \texttt{ijk} at $n = 4000$, and changed the
time of the kernel it could not vectorise by at most 4\% from $n = 200$. At $n = 2000$,
the CSR multiplication was faster than the scalar blocked kernel up to about 55\% density
for uniformly placed nonzeros and 57\% for band matrices, and faster than the vectorised
one up to 33\% and 35\%. The CSR matrices needed less storage than the dense arrays up to
50\% (uniform) and 56\% (band) density, even when the product was almost full. CSR
completed every tested size up to $n = 32\,000$, where uniform inputs with 1\% density
gave a product with 96\% nonzeros and 11.2~GiB of storage. These results describe these
programs on this computer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and objective}

\subsection{Motivation}

In Assignment~1 the \texttt{ijk} multiplication in C followed the $n^3$ model only up to
$n = 1500$: between $n = 1500$ and $2000$ its cost per multiply-add rose from 0.55 to
1.30~ns, which was attributed, without a direct measurement, to the column-by-column
reading of $B$ once $B$ no longer fits in the L3 cache or in the reach of the TLB. If that
explanation is right, changing only the order in which memory is read should remove most
of the jump. A second way to save time is not to do the work at all: a sparse format
stores and multiplies only the nonzeros, but the indexing it needs and the fill-in of the
product can cancel that advantage~\cite{gustavson1978}.

\subsection{Research questions}

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.88\textwidth}
\itshape (A) How do loop reordering, cache blocking and a transposed operand change the
runtime and memory of the \texttt{ijk} dense multiplication of Assignment~1, and how much
of the change comes from compiler vectorisation?

(B) Under which tested densities and nonzero distributions does a CSR sparse
multiplication need less time or memory than a dense method on the same logical matrices?
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\subsection{Variables}

\textbf{Factors varied:} the dense method, the matrix size $n$, the block size $b$ and the
build (scalar or AVX2) in Part~A; the density $d$ of the inputs (the percentage of
nonzero entries; the sparsity is $100\% - d$), the distribution of the nonzeros (uniform
or band), $n$ and the method in Part~B. \textbf{Outcomes:} kernel time, the separately
timed conversion and transposition costs, the nonzeros of the product and the memory.
\textbf{Kept fixed:} language (C), compiler and flags within each build, float64, input
values, machine, single thread.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Dense optimisations and vectorisation}

The triple loop performs $n^3$ multiply-adds whatever the
loop order~\cite{cormen2009}; the order only changes which memory is read in the innermost
loop. In \texttt{ijk} the index $k$ walks down a column of $B$, $8n$ bytes between
consecutive accesses, so every access may touch a different cache line and page.
Interchanging the two inner loops (\texttt{ikj}) keeps $A[i][k]$ in a register and walks
along rows of $B$ and $C$, so every 64-byte line serves 8 consecutive
multiply-adds~\cite{hennessy2019}, but the whole of $B$ is still read once per row of $C$.
\textit{Cache blocking} splits the loops into $b \times b$ tiles, so a tile of $B$ is
reused by all the rows of a tile of $A$ while it is in the cache; the best $b$ depends on
the cache sizes and on the interference between tiles~\cite{lam1991}. Transposing $B$ once,
in $O(n^2)$ operations, instead lets the \texttt{ijk} dot product read both operands with
unit stride.

Floating-point addition is not associative~\cite{goldberg1991}. All the kernels of this
study add the $n$ products of each $C[i][j]$ in increasing $k$, starting from zero, so
they only reorder the visits to different elements and must give results identical bit
for bit to \texttt{ijk}; the dot product of the transposed kernel therefore remains one
serial chain of additions. \textit{Vectorisation} executes one instruction on several
values: an AVX2 instruction works on 4 doubles. gcc vectorises loops with
\texttt{-ftree-vectorize}, which \texttt{-O2} enables with the \texttt{very-cheap} cost
model; \texttt{-fvect-cost-model=dynamic} also allows loops that need a run-time check, for
example that two pointers do not overlap~\cite{gccopt}, and \texttt{-mavx2} allows the AVX2
instructions~\cite{gccx86}. Vectorising the inner loop of \texttt{ikj} over $j$ computes 4
elements of $C$ at once without changing the order of the additions of any of them.

\subsection{Sparse matrices and the CSR format}

The Compressed Sparse Row (CSR) format stores the nonzeros row
by row in two arrays, values and column indices, plus $n + 1$ row offsets~\cite{saad2003}.
With 8-byte values, 4-byte indices and 8-byte offsets, a matrix with $z$ nonzeros needs
$12z + 8(n + 1)$ bytes, against $8n^2$ dense. Gustavson's algorithm computes row $i$ of
$C = AB$ as the sum of the rows $k$ of $B$ scaled by the nonzeros
$A[i][k]$~\cite{gustavson1978}, accumulated in a dense array of $n$ values. For inputs with
density $d$ and uniformly placed nonzeros it performs about $n^3 d^2$ multiply-adds, each
with an indirect access. The product fills in: $C[i][j]$ is nonzero with probability
$1 - (1 - d^2)^n$, 18\% for $d = 1\%$ and 99\% for $d = 5\%$ at $n = 2000$. In a band
matrix the nonzeros are confined to $|i - j| \le w$; the product is confined to
$|i - j| \le 2w$, so its density is bounded by about twice that of the inputs.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Implementations and reused material}

The baseline is the validated C implementation of Assignment~1
(\url{https://github.com/Cafabri/Assigment1}, commit \texttt{0785887}): its kernel, renamed,
and its random generator, seeds, timer, batching rule, processor affinity and
peak-memory measurement are reused unchanged. C was chosen because it was the fastest
implementation of Assignment~1 and its flat row-major array makes the effect of the
access order directly observable; Java and Python were not continued, so that the
comparisons do not mix a language effect with the optimisation and sparse-storage effects.
All kernels are single-threaded and call no numerical library:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{ijk}} (baseline) and \textbf{\texttt{ikj}} (the $j$ and $k$ loops
    interchanged).
    \item \textbf{blocked}: \texttt{ikj} on $b \times b$ tiles, tile loops in the order
    $ii$, $kk$, $jj$; $kk$ runs outside $jj$ in increasing order, so every element still
    receives its products in increasing $k$. Partial edge tiles are handled with bounds.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{transposed}}: the \texttt{ijk} loop on $B^T$, prepared before the
    call; the transposition (\texttt{transpose}) is timed separately.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{csr}}: Gustavson's algorithm (own code). A symbolic pass counts the
    nonzeros of every row of $C$ so that $C$ is allocated once with its exact size; a
    numeric pass accumulates each row in a dense array, with $n$ markers recording which
    columns the row already holds. The column indices of each row are then sorted, by a
    scan of the markers when the row has more than $n/16$ nonzeros, otherwise with
    \texttt{qsort}. The products with a zero factor that the dense kernels add are exactly
    $+0$, so the nonzeros of $C$ are also bit-identical to \texttt{ijk}.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{convert}}: dense $\to$ CSR conversion of $A$ and $B$ (count, then
    copy), timed separately.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Input data}

The values are those of Assignment~1: SplitMix64 with seed 42 for $A$ and 43 for $B$,
uniform in $[0, 10)$. The generator is counter-based, so position $p = i \cdot n + j$ has the
same value whatever the density. The nonzero positions are: \textbf{dense}, all (the
matrices of Assignment~1); \textbf{uniform}, each position independently when a second
SplitMix64 stream (seeds 1042 and 1043) gives a number below $d$; \textbf{band}, positions
with $|i - j| \le w$, $w$ the smallest half-width whose band holds $d n^2$ positions, each
kept with probability $d n^2 / (\mathrm{band\ positions})$ (above 0.95 from $d = 1\%$;
\texttt{results/patterns.csv}). The sparser matrices are subsets of the denser ones. The
sparse method generates its inputs directly in CSR, so large sparse inputs never exist as
dense arrays; the program checks that this gives the same arrays as converting the dense
matrix.

The scalar build keeps the flags of Assignment~1: \texttt{-O2} without \texttt{-O3},
which could interchange the loops by itself~\cite{gccopt}, and \texttt{-ffp-contract=off},
which forbids fused multiply-adds. Both builds use the same source and are compiled with
\texttt{-fopt-info-vec} (\texttt{results/vectorisation\_report.txt}). In the scalar build no
loop of the kernels is vectorised. In the AVX2 build gcc vectorises the inner loops of
\texttt{ikj} and blocked with 32-byte vectors, behind a run-time check that $B$ and $C$ do
not overlap, and the counting loop of the conversion. The dot products of \texttt{ijk} and
\texttt{transposed} are floating-point sums, which gcc only vectorises when allowed to
reorder the additions (\texttt{-fassociative-math})~\cite{gccopt}; \texttt{transposed}
therefore serves as a control without vector code in either build.

\subsection{Measurement protocol}

A pilot run, not reported, was used to choose the sizes and densities.
\texttt{run\_experiment.py} compiles both builds and runs every configuration in a separate
process, never two at the same time, in four experiments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Block size:} blocked, $b \in \{16, 32, 64, 128, 256\}$,
    $n \in \{1000, 2000\}$, both builds. The block size $b^*$ used afterwards is the one
    with the lowest median at $n = 2000$ in each build (declared before running).
    \item \textbf{Dense (Part~A):} \texttt{ijk}, \texttt{ikj}, blocked, \texttt{transposed}
    and \texttt{transpose} (scalar) and \texttt{ikj}, blocked and \texttt{transposed}
    (AVX2), $n \in \{100, 200, 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, 3000, 4000\}$.
    \item \textbf{Density (Part~B):} $n = 2000$,
    $d \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 75, 100\}\%$, both distributions:
    \texttt{csr} and \texttt{convert} (scalar; the CSR kernel has no vectorised loop),
    blocked in both builds, and \texttt{ijk} at $d \in \{0.1, 10, 100\}\%$.
    \item \textbf{Size (Part~B):} \texttt{csr} for $n$ from 1000 to $32\,000$ (doubling) at
    $d \in \{0.1, 1\}\%$, both distributions, and scalar blocked up to $n = 4000$.
\end{itemize}
The \textbf{budget} is 15~minutes per run, as in Assignment~1 (the process is killed:
TIMEOUT), and 16~GiB of matrix storage per process, checked by the program before
allocating (for CSR, after the symbolic pass: MEMORY\_LIMIT); larger sizes of a failed
series are recorded as SKIPPED. The \textbf{timer} (\texttt{QueryPerformanceCounter})
covers only the kernel call. Dense kernels receive their inputs and a zeroed result
prepared outside the timer, as in Assignment~1. The CSR timer includes the symbolic pass
and the allocation and sorting of the output, whose size is unknown in advance; its inputs
are already in CSR, and the conversion is reported separately and added only where
stated. A first call shorter than 10~ms is a separate check call, and the runs are then
timed in batches of at least 10~ms. Each process makes 2 warm-up and 5 measured runs;
configurations whose first process had a median below 60~s were repeated in 3
independent, interleaved processes (15 runs). Each configuration is summarised by the
median and the IQR. Every run writes the checksum of its result, so no call can be
removed by the compiler.

\subsection{Memory measurement}

The \textit{peak working set} of every timing process is measured with
\texttt{GetProcessMemoryInfo}. The \textit{storage} of each method is computed: $24n^2$
bytes for the dense kernels ($32n^2$ with $B^T$); for \texttt{csr}, $12z + 8(n+1)$ bytes per
matrix ($A$, $B$, $C$) plus $12n$ bytes of work space. Each process holds only the arrays
of its own method, so dense and sparse peaks are comparable.

\subsection{Correctness}

Validation was done in four steps:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Exact cases:} at start-up every kernel checks the identity, zero and
    $2 \times 2$ cases and a $7 \times 7$ sparse product with an empty row and partial tiles.
    \item \textbf{Reference implementation:} for $n = 50$ and $257$ (prime, so every block
    size leaves partial tiles) and five inputs (dense; uniform and band at 5\% and 30\%),
    the full result of every kernel, block size and build is compared with NumPy, and the
    nonzeros of the CSR product with SciPy, on independently regenerated inputs. The
    tolerance is a relative error of $10^{-11}$ (the rounding bound
    $nu$~\cite{goldberg1991} is $4.4 \times 10^{-13}$ at $n = 4000$), and reference zeros
    must be exact.
    \item \textbf{Bitwise agreement:} every result of step 2 must be identical to scalar
    \texttt{ijk}.
    \item \textbf{Checksums and nonzeros:} in every configuration the checksum must be the
    same in every run and agree with NumPy/SciPy, and the nonzeros of $A$, $B$ and $C$ must
    equal SciPy's (above $10^8$ nonzeros only the agreement between runs is checked).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Correctness}

All validation steps passed (\texttt{results/correctness.csv},
\texttt{results/checksums.csv}). The largest relative error against NumPy was
$1.9 \times 10^{-15}$ (170 comparisons); the 17 kernels checked were bit-identical to scalar
\texttt{ijk}; the CSR nonzeros equalled SciPy's; and in all 186 configurations the
checksum was identical across runs and within $2.6 \times 10^{-13}$ of the reference (two
without reference). The baseline reproduces Assignment~1: \texttt{ijk} took 0.467, 10.9
and 73.8~s at $n = 1000$, 2000 and 3000, against 0.47, 10.4 and 73.2~s there.

that of \texttt{ijk} rises from 0.52 to 1.36~ns between $n = 1500$ and 2000 and reaches
3.65~ns at $n = 4000$. The blocked kernel keeps 0.219--0.222~ns from $n = 500$ to 4000
(fitted exponent 3.01): it is 1.02--1.16 times faster than \texttt{ikj} up to $n = 1500$,
1.61--1.90 times faster for $n \ge 2000$, and 16.4 times faster than \texttt{ijk} at
$n = 4000$.

\textit{Transposed operand.} \texttt{transposed} takes 0.96--1.02 times the time of
\texttt{ijk} at $n = 200$ and 500 (0.36--0.37~ns in both) and is faster from $n = 1000$
(1.23 times) to 4000 (7.2 times). It is slower than \texttt{ikj} at every size, by 1.6
times for $200 \le n \le 1500$ and 1.2 at $n = 4000$. The transposition takes at most
0.5\% of the kernel time for $n \ge 200$.

\textit{Vectorisation.} The AVX2 build makes the blocked kernel 2.54--2.79 times faster at
every size (0.080~ns per multiply-add from $n = 500$). For \texttt{ikj} the gain falls with
$n$: 2.2--2.8 up to $n = 1500$, 1.59 at $n = 2000$ and 1.43 at $n = 4000$, where the
vectorised \texttt{ikj} needs 3.71 times the time of the vectorised blocked kernel.
\texttt{transposed} takes 0.96--1.00 times its scalar time from $n = 200$ (16\% slower at
$n = 100$). The vectorised blocked kernel is 3.4--6.5 times faster than \texttt{ijk} up to
$n = 1500$ and 17.0, 34.0 and 45.8 times faster at $n = 2000$, 3000 and 4000.

\textit{Variability.} The IQR is at most 3\% of the median in 78.5\% of the 214
configurations. Among medians above 1~ms the largest values are those of the transposition
alone (up to 33.4\%, for calls of a few ms), \texttt{ijk} at $n = 1500$ (9.4\%) and at
$n = 2000$ in the density experiment (up to 13.5\%, process medians up to 22\% apart), and
the vectorised \texttt{ikj} at $n = 1500$ (10.8\%).

\subsection{Part A: memory}

The peak working set is identical for \texttt{ijk}, \texttt{ikj} and blocked (95.6~MiB at
$n = 2000$, 370~MiB at $n = 4000$) and grows by 24.0 bytes per element for $n \ge 1000$,
the three float64 matrices, plus about 4~MiB of fixed program memory. \texttt{transposed}
grows by 32.0 bytes per element (492~MiB at $n = 4000$), because it also holds $B^T$. The
AVX2 build does not change the memory. None of the optimisations needs more memory than
the baseline except the transposed operand.

\textit{Dense methods and fill-in.} The dense kernels do not depend on the zeros: across
the 22 inputs the blocked median varies by 1.6\% in each build, and \texttt{ijk} took
9.8--11.5~s, against 10.9~s on the dense matrices. For uniform inputs the density of $C$
follows the model $1 - (1 - d^2)^n$ (Figure~\ref{fig:fillmem}): 18\% at $d = 1\%$, 55\% at
2\% and 99.3\% at 5\%. For band inputs it is 1.5--2.1 times the input density up to
$d = 20\%$, and reaches 100\% only at $d = 75\%$.

\textit{Time} (Table~\ref{tab:density}, Figure~\ref{fig:densitytime}). CSR is faster than
the scalar blocked kernel up to $d = 50\%$ in both distributions: by 10\,100 (uniform) and
18\,900 (band) times at $d = 0.1\%$, 187 and 1000 times at 1\%, 16.9 and 30.7 at 10\%, and
1.23 and 1.37 at 50\%; at 75\% and 100\% it needs 1.9 and 3.4 times the dense time. The
advantage is larger for band inputs at every density from 0.1\% to 50\%. Interpolating the
ratio in log scale, the crossover is at 55.1\% (uniform) and 57.3\% (band) against the
scalar blocked kernel, and at 32.9\% and 35.3\% against the vectorised one. Divided by
$n^3 d^2$, the CSR time is 0.65--0.76~ns for $d \ge 20\%$ and grows at low density, up to
21.7 (uniform) and 11.6~ns (band) at 0.1\%.

\textit{Conversion.} Converting $A$ and $B$ takes 7.8--53~ms: 45 (uniform) and 85 (band)
times the CSR kernel at $d = 0.1\%$, and less than 20\% of it from $d = 10\%$. Including
it moves the crossovers by at most 1.2 percentage points and leaves CSR about 220 times
faster than the scalar dense kernel at $d = 0.1\%$. The uniform conversion is slowest at
$d = 50\%$ (52.9~ms, against 17.5~ms at 100\%); the band conversion grows steadily with $d$.

\subsection{Evaluation of the hypotheses}

cache remove most of the slowdown of \texttt{ijk} at large $n$ without extra memory, and
vectorisation multiplies the gain of the kernels gcc can vectorise. In answer to

one half (scalar) or one third (vectorised), and needs less storage up to about one half,

\subsection{Effect of the dense optimisations}

The dense kernels perform the same $n^3$ multiply-adds in the same order for each element,
so their differences come only from the order in which memory is read and from the
instructions used. None of the explanations below was measured with hardware counters.

\textbf{Memory access (\texttt{ikj}).}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Up to $n = 1500$, $B$ (at most 18~MB) fits in the 30~MB L3 and \texttt{ikj} is
    1.6--2.1 times faster than \texttt{ijk}. Beyond it, the cost per operation of
    \texttt{ijk} grows 2.6 times and that of \texttt{ikj} only by 40\%.
    \item \texttt{ikj} uses every page and cache line of $B$ completely, so its smaller jump
    is consistent with the L3-capacity explanation of Assignment~1 ($B$, 32~MB at
    $n = 2000$, is read again from memory for every row of $C$); the much larger jump of
    \texttt{ijk} suggests that its stride adds to it. The share of cache and TLB remains a
    hypothesis.
    \item At $n = 2000$ \texttt{ikj} reads one 8-byte element of $B$ per multiply-add,
    equivalent to about 22 (scalar) and 36~GB/s (AVX2), plausible main-memory rates; no
    bandwidth benchmark was run.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Cache blocking.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The blocked kernel removes the dependence on $n$, as expected if each tile of
    $B$ is reused from the cache instead of being read again from memory~\cite{lam1991}.
    \item The best block sizes are consistent with the cache sizes: with $b = 32$ the three
    tiles occupy $3 \times 8b^2 = 24$~KB (48~KB L1), and with $b = 256$ 1.5~MB (2~MB L2).
    \item In the AVX2 build small tiles are 1.8 times slower, consistent with an inner loop
    of $b$ elements too short for the vector loop (4 doubles, unrolled 4 times) and its
    aliasing check.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Transposed operand.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item It gives \texttt{ijk} unit stride but keeps one serial chain of $n$ additions per
    element. Up to $n = 500$, where \texttt{ijk} still finds $B$ in the cache, both cost
    0.36--0.37~ns per multiply-add, while \texttt{ikj}, whose consecutive additions go to
    different elements, costs 0.23~ns: consistent with the dot products being limited by the
    latency of the additions~\cite{hennessy2019}.
    \item The transposition costs at most 0.5\% of the kernel, so it is a valid fix for the
    memory problem of \texttt{ijk}, but not as good as reordering the loops.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Vectorisation.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The loop order is what allows it: \texttt{ikj} updates independent elements and is
    vectorised; the dot product cannot be without changing the rounding, and
    \texttt{transposed} gains nothing.
    \item The gain never reaches the 4 doubles of a register (at most 2.8), and in
    \texttt{ikj} it falls to 1.4 at large $n$: with faster arithmetic, the kernel that reads
    $B$ from memory for every row waits longer for memory, which blocking avoids.
    \item The optimisations combine: over \texttt{ijk} at $n = 4000$, blocking alone gives
    16.4 times, vectorisation with the loop reordering it needs 12.4 times, and both 45.8
    times.
    \item The 16\% slowdown of \texttt{transposed} at $n = 100$ in the AVX2 build is not
    explained by the measurements; code alignment is one possible cause.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Effect of sparse storage}

\textbf{Time and crossover.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item CSR performs only about $n^3 d^2$ of the $n^3$ multiply-adds but pays more for
    each: for $d \ge 20\%$ its time divided by $n^3 d^2$ (0.65--0.76~ns) is 3.0--3.5 times
    the 0.22~ns of the scalar blocked kernel and 8--9.5 times the 0.080~ns of the vectorised
    one, because each product reads a column index, updates the accumulator at a scattered
    position and checks a marker.
    \item If both costs were constant, the crossover would be where $d^2$ equals their
    ratio: $\sqrt{0.22 / 0.72} \approx 55\%$ (scalar) and $\sqrt{0.080 / 0.72} \approx 33\%$
    (vectorised), close to the measured 55\% and 33\% (uniform). The crossover therefore
    depends on how optimised the dense alternative is.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Low density and distribution.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item At low density the cost per product grows, because the products fall as $d^2$
    while the costs per row of $C$ (marker initialisation, sorting) and per nonzero of $C$
    do not.
    \item This is why the distribution matters: at $d = 1\%$ both perform about the same
    number of products, but the uniform product has 18\% nonzeros and the band product 2\%,
    and the band kernel is 5.4 times faster. The adjacent rows of $B$ selected by a band row
    may also help; this was not separated from the fill-in.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Conversion.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Negligible at medium and high densities. At $d = 0.1\%$ it is bounded below by
    reading the two dense matrices twice (7.8~ms against 0.17~ms for the kernel), and CSR
    still remains about 220 times faster.
    \item Its peak at $d = 50\%$ for uniform inputs is consistent with the branch on
    \texttt{v != 0} being unpredictable when half of the random entries are zero, unlike
    band inputs, whose nonzeros form almost contiguous runs; this was not measured.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Memory.} P6 compared the 12 bytes of a CSR nonzero with the 8 of a dense entry for
the product only. The relevant comparison is with all three dense matrices: even when $C$
is full and its CSR form needs $12n^2$ bytes, the sparse $A$ and $B$ save more than that up
to about $d = 50\%$.

\textbf{Scaling with $n$.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Band inputs (exponent 2.5): fixed per-row costs dominate at small $n$ and are
    amortised; the time per product of band 1\% falls from 3.8 to 0.71~ns between
    $n = 1000$ and $32\,000$.
    \item Uniform 0.1\% (exponent 3.33): the rows of $C$ have about $d^2 n^2$ nonzeros
    (about 1000 at $n = 32\,000$), sorted with \texttt{qsort}, so the cost per product grows
    with $\log n$; not confirmed with a profile.
    \item Limit of sparse storage: at $n = 32\,000$ the uniform 1\% product has 96\%
    nonzeros and its CSR storage (11.2~GiB) exceeds the 7.6~GiB of a dense $C$, while the
    band product needed 473~MiB. Three dense matrices would need 22.9~GiB, above the
    budget, and the cubic extrapolation of the blocked kernel gives about 2~hours per run;
    neither was run.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Observed bottlenecks}

Inferred from the timings, without hardware counters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{ijk}:} the strided reads of $B$ once $B$ no longer fits in the L3
    (up to 3.65~ns per multiply-add at $n = 4000$).
    \item \textbf{\texttt{ikj} (both builds):} reading the whole of $B$ from main memory for
    every row of $C$ for $n \ge 2000$; the faster the arithmetic, the larger its share.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{transposed}, and \texttt{ijk} while $B$ fits in the cache:} the
    latency of one serial chain of additions per element.
    \item \textbf{Blocked kernel:} neither memory nor $n$; its constant cost is set by the
    arithmetic, which only vectorisation reduced (0.22 to 0.080~ns).
    \item \textbf{CSR kernel:} the indirect access of every product; at low density, the
    per-row costs and the sorting of $C$; with uniform inputs, the fill-in of $C$, which
    also sets its memory (11.2~GiB at $n = 32\,000$, the closest to the budget).
    \item \textbf{Conversion:} reading the dense arrays, which dominates only below about
    1\% density.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item One computer, compiler and set of flags; other caches would move the jumps, the
    best block size and the crossovers. The power plan was \textit{Balanced}, not a fixed
    frequency.
    \item Only five square block sizes were tried, without register blocking, so the
    blocked kernel is not the fastest possible dense kernel; the crossovers are
    interpolated between 11 densities.
    \item The cache, TLB, latency and branch-prediction explanations are inferred from
    timings only, without hardware counters or profiles.
    \item Part~B used $n = 2000$ and two distributions; the crossovers depend on $n$
    through the fill-in, and other patterns may behave differently. The CSR code was not
    compared with an optimised library, and its sorting rule was not tuned.
    \item \texttt{ijk} at $n \ge 3000$ ran in one process, and \texttt{ijk} at $n = 2000$
    showed process medians up to 22\% apart. No configuration reached the budget, so the
    actual largest feasible sizes were not determined.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}

For the programs and the conditions described in Section~3:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Changing only the order of memory accesses makes the dense multiplication much
    faster with the same operations and memory: \texttt{ikj} is 1.6--8.6 times faster
    than \texttt{ijk} for $500 \le n \le 4000$ and the blocked kernel 1.7--16.4 times,
    with a constant 0.22~ns per multiply-add and without the jump of Assignment~1.
    \item Transposing $B$ removes most of the slowdown of \texttt{ijk} at large $n$, at a
    negligible cost and one more matrix of memory, but stays 1.2--1.6 times slower than
    \texttt{ikj}.
    \item Vectorisation multiplies the effect of the optimisations it applies to: 2.5--2.8
    times for the blocked kernel, 45.8 times faster than \texttt{ijk} at $n = 4000$, and
    nothing for the dot-product kernel.
    \item At $n = 2000$, CSR is faster than the dense blocked kernel up to about 55--57\%
    density (scalar) and 33--35\% (vectorised), and needs less storage up to 50--56\%. Its
    advantage is larger when the product stays sparse (band) than when it fills in
    (uniform), and the conversion only matters at very low densities.
\end{itemize}
The optimisations make each operation cheaper; sparse storage avoids operations at a higher
cost per operation, so its advantage depends on the density, on the distribution of the
nonzeros (through the fill-in of the product) and on how fast the dense alternative is,
not on the percentage of zeros alone. Open questions are the share of the cache and of address translation in
the slowdown of the dense kernels, which hardware counters could answer, and how the
crossovers move with $n$ and with several threads.
