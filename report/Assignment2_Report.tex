\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This study measures how loop reordering, cache blocking, a transposed operand and compiler
vectorisation change the time and memory of the triple-loop C multiplication of
Assignment~1, and when a CSR sparse multiplication is faster or smaller than a dense one.
All kernels are single-threaded and bit-identical, timed on an Intel Core i7-13700KF (median
of 5--15 runs). Cache blocking was 16.4$\times$ faster than the baseline at $n = 4000$. AVX2
made it a further 2.5--2.8$\times$ faster with each build's best block size, and
2.19$\times$ with the same block size at $n = 2000$. At $n = 2000$ and with uniform
nonzeros, CSR beat the scalar blocked kernel up to a density between 50 and 75\% (about 55\%
by log interpolation) and the vectorised one up to about 33\%. Its storage stayed smaller up
to about 50\%. The fill-in of the product, not the share of zeros alone, decides its
advantage.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and objective}

In Assignment~1 the \texttt{ijk} multiplication in C followed the $n^3$ model only up to
$n = 1500$: between $n = 1500$ and $2000$ its cost per multiply-add rose from 0.55 to
1.30~ns, attributed, without a direct measurement, to the column-by-column reading of $B$
once it no longer fits in the L3 cache or the reach of the TLB. If so, changing only the
order in which memory is read should remove most of the jump. Alternatively, a sparse format
stores and multiplies only the nonzeros, but its indexing and the fill-in of the product can
cancel that advantage~\cite{gustavson1978}. The study asks two questions:

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.92\textwidth}
\itshape (A) How do loop reordering, cache blocking and a transposed operand change the
runtime and memory of the \texttt{ijk} dense multiplication of Assignment~1, and how much of
the change comes from compiler vectorisation?

(B) Under which tested densities and nonzero distributions does a CSR sparse multiplication
need less time or memory than a dense method on the same logical matrices?
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\textbf{Factors varied:} dense method, size $n$, block size $b$ and build (Part~A); density
$d$ of the inputs (percentage of nonzero entries; the sparsity is $100\% - d$), distribution
of the nonzeros (uniform or band), $n$ and method (Part~B). \textbf{Outcomes:} kernel time,
the separately timed conversion and transposition, the nonzeros of the product and the
memory. \textbf{Kept fixed:} language, compiler and flags within each build, float64, input
values, machine, one thread.

\section{Background}

\textbf{Dense optimisations.} The triple loop performs $n^3$ multiply-adds whatever the loop
order~\cite{cormen2009}; the order only changes which memory the innermost loop reads. In
\texttt{ijk} the index $k$ walks down a column of $B$, $8n$ bytes between accesses.
Interchanging the inner loops (\texttt{ikj}) walks along rows of $B$ and $C$, so every
64-byte line serves 8 multiply-adds~\cite{hennessy2019}, but all of $B$ is still read once
per row of $C$. \textit{Cache blocking} splits the loops into $b \times b$ tiles, so a tile
of $B$ is reused while it is cached; the best $b$ depends on the caches~\cite{lam1991}.
Transposing $B$ once, in $O(n^2)$, lets the \texttt{ijk} dot product read both operands with
unit stride.

\textbf{Rounding and vectorisation.} Floating-point addition is not
associative~\cite{goldberg1991}, so all kernels add the products of each $C[i][j]$ in
increasing $k$ and give results bit-identical to \texttt{ijk}. \textit{Vectorisation}
executes one instruction on several values: 4 doubles with AVX2, 2 with SSE2. gcc vectorises
loops at \texttt{-O2} with a \texttt{very-cheap} cost model;
\texttt{-fvect-cost-model=dynamic} also allows loops that need a run-time overlap
check~\cite{gccopt}, and \texttt{-mavx2} allows AVX2 instructions~\cite{gccx86}. The inner
loop of \texttt{ikj} can be vectorised over $j$ without changing any sum; a dot product
cannot, unless the additions may be reordered.

\textbf{Sparse matrices and CSR.} The Compressed Sparse Row format stores the nonzeros row by
row (values and column indices) plus $n + 1$ row offsets~\cite{saad2003}: with 8-byte
values, 4-byte indices and 8-byte offsets, $12z + 8(n + 1)$ bytes for $z$ nonzeros, against
$8n^2$ dense. Gustavson's algorithm computes row $i$ of $C = AB$ as the sum of the rows $k$
of $B$ scaled by the nonzeros $A[i][k]$~\cite{gustavson1978}: about $n^3 d^2$ multiply-adds
for density $d$, each with an indirect access. The product fills in: for uniform nonzeros
$C[i][j]$ is nonzero with probability $1 - (1 - d^2)^n$ (99\% for $d = 5\%$ at
$n = 2000$), while a band product ($|i - j| \le 2w$ for inputs with $|i - j| \le w$) has at
most about twice the input density.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Implementations and reused material}

The baseline is the validated C implementation of Assignment~1
(\url{https://github.com/Cafabri/Assigment1}, commit \texttt{0785887}): its kernel, renamed,
and its random generator, seeds, timer, batching rule, processor affinity and peak-memory
measurement are reused unchanged. Only C was continued (the fastest language of
Assignment~1), so that no language effect mixes with the optimisation and sparse-storage
effects. All kernels are single-threaded own code and call no numerical library:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{ijk}} (baseline); \textbf{\texttt{ikj}} (the $j$ and $k$ loops
    interchanged); \textbf{blocked}, \texttt{ikj} on $b \times b$ tiles with tile loops
    $ii$, $kk$, $jj$ ($kk$ outside $jj$, so each element still receives its products in
    increasing $k$; partial edge tiles handled with bounds); \textbf{\texttt{transposed}},
    the \texttt{ijk} loop on $B^T$, prepared before the call (the transposition,
    \texttt{transpose}, is timed separately).
    \item \textbf{\texttt{csr}}: Gustavson's algorithm. A symbolic pass counts the nonzeros
    of each row of $C$, so $C$ is allocated once; a numeric pass accumulates each row in a
    dense array with $n$ markers, and sorts its column indices by a scan of the markers when
    the row has more than $n/16$ nonzeros, otherwise with \texttt{qsort}. The nonzeros of $C$
    are bit-identical to \texttt{ijk}, whose zero factors add only exact $+0$.
    \textbf{\texttt{convert}}: dense $\to$ CSR conversion of $A$ and $B$, timed separately.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Input data}

Values are those of Assignment~1: SplitMix64 with seed 42 for $A$ and 43 for $B$, uniform
in $[0, 10)$. The generator is counter-based, so position $p = i \cdot n + j$ has the same
value at every density. Nonzero positions: \textbf{dense}, all (the matrices of
Assignment~1); \textbf{uniform}, each position independently when a second SplitMix64 stream
(seeds 1042 and 1043) gives a number below $d$; \textbf{band}, positions with
$|i - j| \le w$, $w$ the smallest half-width whose band holds $d n^2$ positions, each kept
with probability $d n^2 / (\mathrm{band\ positions})$ (above 0.95 from $d = 1\%$;
\texttt{results/patterns.csv}). Sparser matrices are subsets of denser ones. The sparse
method generates its inputs directly in CSR, so large sparse inputs never exist as dense
arrays; the program checks that this equals converting the dense matrix.

\subsection{Environment}

\textbf{CPU:} Intel Core i7-13700KF, 8 P-cores with 2 threads each (logical processors
0--15) and 8 E-cores; 48~KB L1 data and 2~MB L2 per P-core, 30~MB shared L3; 31.8~GiB RAM;
Windows 11 Pro, power plan \textit{Balanced}. The runner and every benchmark process are
restricted to the P-cores. \textbf{Compiler:} gcc 16.1.0 (MSYS2/MinGW64) with three builds:
\texttt{scalar}, \texttt{-O2 -ffp-contract=off} (the flags of Assignment~1); \texttt{sse2},
which adds \texttt{-fvect-cost-model=dynamic}; and \texttt{avx2}, which also adds
\texttt{-mavx2}. \textbf{Analysis:} CPython 3.11.9, NumPy 2.2 and SciPy 1.15 (reference
results), pandas 2.2, matplotlib 3.10 (\texttt{results/*/environment.txt}). \texttt{-O3},
which could interchange the loops by itself, and fused multiply-adds are avoided in every
build~\cite{gccopt}. The gcc reports (\texttt{-fopt-info-vec},
\texttt{results/*/vectorisation\_report.txt}) show no vectorised kernel loop in the scalar
build; the inner loops of \texttt{ikj} and blocked (and the counting loop of the conversion)
vectorised with 16-byte (SSE2) or 32-byte (AVX2) vectors behind a run-time overlap check;
and the dot products of \texttt{ijk} and \texttt{transposed} vectorised in no build (that
needs \texttt{-fassociative-math}~\cite{gccopt}), so \texttt{transposed} is a control.

\subsection{Measurement protocol}

\texttt{run\_experiment.py} compiles the builds and runs each configuration in a separate
process, never two at once. \textbf{Block size:} blocked, $b \in \{16, 32, 64, 128, 256\}$,
$n \in \{1000, 2000\}$; the $b^*$ of each build is the one with the lowest median at
$n = 2000$ (rule fixed before running). \textbf{Dense (Part~A):} \texttt{ijk}, \texttt{ikj},
blocked, \texttt{transposed} and \texttt{transpose} (scalar), \texttt{ikj}, blocked and
\texttt{transposed} (AVX2), $n \in \{100, 200, 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, 3000, 4000\}$. This
sequence was fixed before measuring and ends at $n = 4000$ by design, to keep the whole
study at about 4~hours of benchmark processes. \textbf{Density (Part~B):} $n = 2000$,
$d \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 75, 100\}\%$, both distributions: \texttt{csr}
and \texttt{convert} (scalar; the CSR kernel has no vectorised loop), blocked in both builds,
and \texttt{ijk} at $d \in \{0.1, 10, 100\}\%$. \textbf{Size (Part~B):} \texttt{csr} for
$n = 1000$ to $32\,000$ (doubling) at $d \in \{0.1, 1\}\%$, both distributions, and scalar
blocked up to $n = 4000$.

A \textbf{second measurement session (27 October 2026)}, with the same programs, protocol
and budget (\texttt{run\_experiment.py -{}-extension}, 46.2~min, \texttt{results/extension/}),
added: the \texttt{sse2} build, which separates enabling vectorisation from the vector
width (block sweep, and \texttt{ikj} and blocked with its $b^*$ at every dense size); series
up to the budget (\texttt{csr} at $n = 64\,000$ for the four size inputs, and scalar
\texttt{ijk} at $n = 8000$, whose run time a cubic extrapolation placed beyond 15~minutes,
an estimate, not a measurement); and three
drift references at $n = 2000$ (scalar and AVX2 blocked, scalar \texttt{ikj}) to compare
the two sessions.

The \textbf{budget} is 15~minutes per run, as in Assignment~1 (the process is killed:
TIMEOUT), and 16~GiB of matrix storage per process, checked before allocating (for CSR,
after the symbolic pass: MEMORY\_LIMIT); larger sizes of a failed series are SKIPPED.
\textbf{Repetitions:} each process makes 2 warm-up and 5 measured runs; configurations whose
first process had a median below 60~s are repeated in 3 interleaved processes (15 runs).
Each configuration is summarised by the median and the IQR. A first call shorter than 10~ms
is a separate check call, and the runs are then timed in batches of at least 10~ms (time per
call reported).

The \textbf{timer} (\texttt{QueryPerformanceCounter}) covers only the kernel call, with
different boundaries for the two families: dense kernels receive their inputs and a zeroed
$C$ prepared outside it, as in Assignment~1, while the CSR timer includes the symbolic pass,
the allocation and sorting of $C$ and, in batched runs, freeing the previous outputs. This
asymmetry favours the dense kernels.
The CSR inputs are already in CSR; the conversion is reported separately and added only
where stated. Every check call and unbatched run writes the checksum of its result, so no
call can be removed by the compiler.

\textbf{Memory.} \texttt{GetProcessMemoryInfo} gives the \textit{peak working set} of each
process, which holds only the arrays of its own method. The \textit{storage} is computed:
$24n^2$ bytes for the dense kernels ($32n^2$ with $B^T$); for \texttt{csr}, $12z + 8(n+1)$
bytes per matrix ($A$, $B$, $C$) plus $12n$ bytes of work space.

\subsection{Correctness}

(1)~At start-up every kernel checks identity, zero, $2 \times 2$ and $7 \times 7$ sparse
cases with an empty row and partial tiles. (2)~For $n = 50$ and 257 (prime, so every $b$
leaves partial tiles) and five inputs (dense; uniform and band at 5\% and 30\%), every
kernel, block size and build is compared with NumPy, and the CSR nonzeros with SciPy, on
independently regenerated inputs (relative tolerance $10^{-11}$, above the rounding bound
$nu$~\cite{goldberg1991} of $4.4 \times 10^{-13}$ at $n = 4000$; reference zeros exact).
(3)~These results must equal scalar \texttt{ijk} bit for bit. (4)~In every configuration the
checksum must be identical across runs and agree with NumPy/SciPy, and the nonzeros of $A$,
$B$ and $C$ must equal SciPy's (above $10^8$ nonzeros only agreement between runs).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Correctness and variability}

All checks passed (\texttt{results/correctness.csv}, \texttt{checksums.csv}). The largest
error against NumPy was $1.9 \times 10^{-15}$, and all 25 kernels were bit-identical to
scalar \texttt{ijk}. The checksums were identical across runs in all 205 configurations. The
baseline reproduces Assignment~1: \texttt{ijk} took 10.9~s at $n = 2000$, against 10.4~s
there.

The IQR is at most 3\% of the median in 78.5\% of the configurations of the first session
and in all of the second. The largest IQR is that of the transposition alone (up to 33.4\%,
calls of a few ms). The process medians of \texttt{ijk} at $n = 2000$ (10.49--11.09~s) make
the speedups at that size uncertain by about 10\%.

\subsection{Part A: dense methods}

\textit{Block size} (Table~\ref{tab:blocksize}). The rule gives $b^* = 32$ (scalar), 64
(SSE2) and 256 (AVX2). At $n = 2000$ the scalar differences are small and not monotonic, with
the worst $b$ 1.19$\times$ slower. In the AVX2 build the time falls as $b$ grows, and
$b = 16$ is 1.85$\times$ slower than $b = 256$.

\textit{Drift between sessions.} Three references were repeated at $n = 2000$, with 15 runs
in each session. The AVX2 blocked median moved by $+0.1\%$, inside the first IQR. The scalar
blocked median moved by $-0.4\%$ (1.769 to 1.762~s), and scalar \texttt{ikj} by $-4.2\%$
(2.842 to 2.724~s). Ratios between the SSE2 build and the other two are therefore uncertain
by under 1\% for the blocked kernel and up to about 4\% for \texttt{ikj}.

\textit{Memory.} The peak working set of \texttt{ijk}, \texttt{ikj} and blocked is identical
in every build: 24.0 bytes per element, the three float64 matrices. \texttt{transposed}
needs 32.0 bytes per element, because it also holds $B^T$.

\subsection{Part B: density at $n = 2000$}

\textit{Dense methods and fill-in.} The dense kernels do not depend on the zeros: across the
22 inputs, the blocked median varies by 1.6\% in each build (Table~\ref{tab:density}). For
uniform inputs the density of $C$ follows $1 - (1 - d^2)^n$ and reaches 99.3\% at $d = 5\%$.
For band inputs it stays about twice the input density and reaches 100\% only at $d = 75\%$
(Figure~\ref{fig:fillmem}, left).

\textit{Time} (Table~\ref{tab:density}, Figure~\ref{fig:densitytime}). CSR is faster than
the scalar blocked kernel up to $d = 50\%$ in both distributions. Its advantage is larger for
band inputs at every density below the crossover, for example 1000$\times$ against
187$\times$ at $d = 1\%$. Against the scalar blocked kernel, the crossover lies between 50
and 75\% density (about 55\% uniform and 57\% band by log interpolation). Against the AVX2
kernel it lies between 30 and 50\% (about 33\% and 35\%). Divided by $n^3 d^2$, the CSR time
is 0.65--0.84~ns for $d \ge 20\%$ but grows at low density, up to 26.6~ns (uniform,
$d = 0.5\%$).

\textit{Conversion.} Converting $A$ and $B$ takes 7.8--53~ms. At $d = 0.1\%$ this is
45$\times$ (uniform) and 85$\times$ (band) the CSR kernel time. From $d = 10\%$ it is under
20\% of it. Including it moves the crossovers by at most 1.2 percentage points.

\textit{Memory} (Figure~\ref{fig:fillmem}, right). The CSR storage of $A$, $B$ and $C$ stays
below the $24n^2$ bytes of the three dense matrices up to about 50\% density (uniform) and
56\% (band). At 100\% it is 1.5$\times$ larger. At $d = 5\%$, with an almost full product,
the uniform CSR matrices still need 0.55$\times$ the dense storage. The CSR peak working set

\subsection{Effect of the dense optimisations}

The dense kernels perform the same operations in the same order, so their differences come
only from memory access order and instructions. The explanations below are hypotheses
consistent with the timings; no hardware counters or profiler were used.

\textbf{Memory access.} At $n = 2000$, $B$ (32~MB) no longer fits in the 30~MB L3. Between
$n = 1500$ and 2000, the cost per operation of \texttt{ijk} grows 2.6$\times$ and that of
\texttt{ikj} only by 40\%. Since \texttt{ikj} uses every cache line of $B$ fully, its smaller
jump is consistent with the L3-capacity explanation of Assignment~1: $B$ is read again from
memory for every row of $C$, at about 22.5~GB/s. The larger jump of \texttt{ijk} suggests
that its stride adds to it; the shares of cache and TLB remain open.

\textbf{Cache blocking} removes the dependence on $n$, as expected if tiles of $B$ are reused
from the cache~\cite{lam1991}. Three tiles take 24~KB with $b = 32$ (48~KB L1) and 1.5~MB
with $b = 256$ (2~MB L2). Small tiles may be too short for the AVX2 vector loop.

\textbf{Transposed operand.} At $n = 200$ and 500, where $B$ still fits in the cache,
\texttt{ijk} and \texttt{transposed} both cost 0.34--0.37~ns per multiply-add, and
\texttt{ikj}, whose consecutive additions go to different elements, 0.23~ns. This is
consistent with dot products limited by the latency of one serial chain of
additions~\cite{hennessy2019}.

\textbf{Vectorisation.} The loop order is what allows it: \texttt{ikj} updates independent
elements and is vectorised, the dot product is not. Over \texttt{ijk} at $n = 4000$, blocking
alone gives 16.4$\times$ and vectorisation with the loop reordering it needs 12.4$\times$.
Combined, they give 45.8$\times$. Part of the blocked gain comes from the larger $b^*$ of the
vector builds (2.19$\times$ at fixed $b$ against 2.76$\times$). For the blocked kernel,
54--56\% of the gain (in log scale) comes from enabling vectorisation and the rest from the
wider vectors. For \texttt{ikj} at $n \ge 3000$, SSE2 already obtains 87--89\% of it. This is
consistent with \texttt{ikj} being limited by memory there, where wider arithmetic would not
shorten the wait. P4 holds, with that refinement.

\subsection{Effect of sparse storage}

\textbf{Crossover.} Unlike the dense optimisations, which keep the $n^3$ operations, CSR
removes operations but pays more for each. For $d \ge 20\%$ its cost per product is
2.9--3.8$\times$ that of the scalar blocked kernel and 8.1--10$\times$ that of the vectorised
one (a column index, a marker check and a scattered update per product). A constant-cost
model, with the uniform CSR cost at $d = 50\%$ (0.717~ns), puts the crossover where $d^2$
equals the ratio of costs. It is coherent with both measured crossovers, scalar and AVX2, to
within one percentage point. As it is calibrated at $d = 50\%$, next to the crossover, this
is a consistency check, not an independent validation; the crossover depends on the dense
alternative.

\textbf{Low density and distribution.} At low density the cost per product grows, because
the products fall as $d^2$ while the costs per row and per nonzero of $C$ do not. The uniform
peak at $d = 0.5\%$ is consistent with the sorting rule. There the rows of $C$ have about 96
nonzeros, fewer than $n/16 = 125$, and are sorted with \texttt{qsort}; at $d = 1\%$ a scan of
the markers is used. The distribution acts through the fill-in. At $d = 1\%$ both perform
about the same number of products, but the uniform product has 18\% nonzeros and the band
product 2\%, and the band kernel is 5.4$\times$ faster.

\textbf{Conversion and memory.} At $d = 0.1\%$ the conversion is bounded below by reading the
two dense matrices. Its uniform peak at $d = 50\%$ is consistent with an unpredictable branch
on \texttt{v~!=~0}. P6 compared only the product: a full CSR $C$ needs 12 bytes per entry
against 8 for a dense $C$. Over all three matrices, the CSR total stays below the dense total
up to about 50\% density (uniform), because $A$ and $B$ are sparse.

\textbf{Scaling with $n$ and limit.} For band inputs, fixed per-row costs are amortised: the
time per product of band 1\% falls from 3.83 to 0.716~ns up to $n = 32\,000$. For uniform
0.1\% it grows from 13.2 to 44.2~ns, consistent with \texttt{qsort} on rows that stay below
$n/16$ nonzeros. Its fall to 32.9~ns at $n = 64\,000$ is not explained by this. The limit of
CSR is set by the nonzeros of the product. At $n = 32\,000$ the uniform 1\% product has 96\%
nonzeros, so almost all of the 11.2~GiB of CSR storage is $C$, against 22.9~GiB for three
dense matrices. At $n = 64\,000$ the CSR total would need 46.6~GiB, and three dense matrices
91.6~GiB. No dense run was attempted at $n = 32\,000$: cubic extrapolation of the blocked
kernel gives about 2.0~hours per run (estimated, not measured).

\subsection{Observed bottlenecks}

Inferred from the timings: \texttt{ijk}, the strided reads of $B$ beyond the L3;
\texttt{ikj}, reading $B$ from memory for every row of $C$ ($n \ge 2000$);
\texttt{transposed}, the latency of one serial chain of additions; blocked, the arithmetic,
reduced only by vectorisation (0.22 to 0.080~ns); CSR, the indirect access of every product,
the sorting of $C$ at low density and the fill-in of $C$, which sets its memory; the
conversion, reading the dense arrays (dominant only below about 1\% density).

\subsection{Limitations}

One computer, compiler and set of flags, without fixed frequency; the cache, TLB, latency,
sorting and branch explanations are inferred from timings only. Five square block sizes,
without register blocking. The crossovers are interpolated between 11 densities,
at one size and for two distributions, and depend on $n$ through the fill-in. The CSR and
dense timers have different boundaries (Section~3.4). \texttt{ijk} at $n \ge 3000$ ran in
one process. The drift references moved by up to 4.2\% between sessions, which bounds
comparisons across them. The largest feasible size was bracketed only for \texttt{ijk}
(4000 to 8000) and CSR uniform 1\% ($32\,000$ to $64\,000$).

\section{Conclusions}

For these programs, inputs and machine:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reordering memory accesses makes the dense multiplication much faster with the same
    operations and memory, and blocking removes the jump of Assignment~1.
    \item Vectorisation multiplies the effect of the optimisations it applies to. Wider
    vectors help the blocked kernel but hardly \texttt{ikj} at large $n$, consistent with
    \texttt{ikj} being limited by memory there.
    \item At $n = 2000$, CSR beats the scalar blocked kernel up to a density between 50 and
    75\% (about 55\% by log interpolation), and the vectorised one up to about a third. Its
    size limit is set by the fill-in of the product: under 16~GiB, the uniform 1\% inputs ran
    only up to $n = 32\,000$.
\end{itemize}
Sparse storage saves operations at a higher cost per operation: its advantage depends on the
density, the nonzero distribution and the dense alternative, not on the share of zeros alone.

\textbf{Open questions.} The cache/TLB split of the dense slowdowns (no hardware counters were
used); the drop of the uniform 0.1\% cost at $n = 64\,000$; a comparison with an optimised
sparse library; and the untuned sorting threshold of $n/16$ nonzeros per row.
